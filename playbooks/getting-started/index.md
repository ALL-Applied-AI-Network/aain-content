# Getting Started

How to open the year so people stay, run two tracks all semester, and hand the chapter on so it outlives you.

**New club, or one that already runs?** Start where you are.

| You are | Start at |
|---|---|
| Starting from nothing | [Week 1](#week-1), then a hackathon or an Innovation Lab as your first project |
| Already running 40 to 100 members | [First semester](#first-semester). Claim your chapter and bring your roster in with Invite/Import Members |

## Start here

1. The first three weeks decide the year: org fair (week 0), say the whole year out loud (week 1), a great speaker by week 3, projects kicked off by week 5.
2. Run two tracks at once: an hour-long event every two weeks in a slot that never moves, and at least one hands-on project a semester.
3. Write down how you run things as you go, and recruit next year's board in the spring, not after graduation.

## Quick guide

**A quiet start kills a year. Advertise hard in the first three weeks, because that window doesn't come back.**

### The semester, week by week

| Week | What happens |
|---|---|
| **0** | **Org fair.** Talk about the club, get people onto an interest list, get them saying they'll come to the first event. |
| **1** | **"What is [your club]?"** The whole plan for the year, out loud. *Bring food if you possibly can.* |
| **2** | **"Getting a hands-on project."** Why to join one, how, and what actually happens. *Food again.* |
| **3** | **Your first real event.** Make it a great speaker. *Food again.* |
| **4** | Another real event, or your first break week if you need one. |
| **5** | **The kick-off.** Sign-ups closed earlier in the week; this is the reveal of who's on which team and who's mentoring them. |
| **6+** | Bi-weekly speaker events and workshops. Weekly is fine if you can sustain it, the largest orgs typically run one a week. |

### Two things run at once

| Track | What it is | How often |
|---|---|---|
| **The pulse** | A speaker or a workshop, about an hour, after classes | At least every two weeks |
| **The depth** | A hands-on project: an Innovation Lab, a Research Group or a hackathon | At least one a semester, kicked off in week 5 |

They aren't a ladder. MSOE has had research groups, Innovation Labs and a hackathon running in the same semester.

### What to say, every time

- There's a **wider community** here. It's how most people come through the door.
- **Distinguished speakers** who want to talk to *you*, booked regularly.
- It has **landed people jobs**, more often than not.
- It still **takes hard work**. Saying so is why the rest gets believed.
- **A project that matters**, with upperclassmen on your team.
- **A serious portfolio piece before Christmas break.**
- **You do not need experience.** Say it every time; most students assume the opposite.

**Don't scope it to CS.** MSOE graduates fewer than 100 CS students a year, and its club passed 500 members. The growth came from software and computer engineering, then UX, business and mechanical engineering.

**Food fills a room** and empties a budget. Find a deal or get pizza. Overlap dinner if you can feed people; if you can't, start at 6:00 or 6:30.

## Week 1

**Before you plan a semester, prove there's demand and line up a team. Skip this if your club already runs.**

### How we did it at MAIC

The club was three years old before it grew. The turnaround started with a one-page audit: upperclassmen thought it was for freshmen, freshmen thought it was another class, and faculty and companies didn't know how to plug in. Every change that followed answered a line on that page.

### The repeatable version

1. **Find the gap.** List every tech org on campus and what each one leaves out. That gap is your opening.
2. **Talk to 20 students individually**, not just friends. Would they come to a club where they build real AI projects? If fewer than half say yes, sharpen the pitch.
3. **Run a pilot event.** One room, one concrete build. If 10+ people show up and stay engaged for an hour, you have your answer.
4. **Lock in 2–4 co-founders** with different skills. Solo founders burn out, and an operations partner matters more than another coder.
5. **Say what you're not:** not a math study group, not a lecture series, not only for CS majors.

## First semester

**Your rhythm is the product: one slot for the pulse, a project kicked off in week 5, and food at the first three events.**

### How we did it at MAIC

Every other Thursday, 6:30 to 7:30, the same room all year, with free food every time. Before that, the slot moved with the board's schedules, and every move cost people who never came back.

### The repeatable version

1. **Pick a day, time, and room**, then never change them. Bi-weekly is the sweet spot. One moved meeting costs about 15% of attendance that doesn't come back.
2. **Copy the opening weeks** from the [Quick guide](#quick-guide). Week 5 is a reveal: teams, mentors and the sponsor in one room.
3. **Recruit in parallel:** first-year seminars, 30 seconds at the start of relevant lectures, one group chat on every poster, and "bring a friend".
4. **Feed people.** Sponsor money or your student org budget. It's the best money you'll spend.

**In the dashboard:**

| Job | Where |
|---|---|
| Put an event on the calendar | **Events → New Event**: Speaker, Workshop or Social for the pulse; Hackathon, Innovation Lab or Research Group for the depth |
| Take attendance | The event's **QR** tile. Enlarge it on the projector and people scan |
| Bring your roster in | **Invite/Import Members** on Home or Members: a sign-up QR, a spreadsheet, or another platform |
| Ask for a speaker, judges, a venue or money | **Sponsors → Requests**, or **Request From the ALL Network** inside New Event |
| Show it publicly | Your **Website** lists every published event, your officers and the leaderboard |

## Year two

**Year one is founding. Year two is whether the chapter survives its first handoff.**

### How we did it at MAIC

Seven months in, the president who rebuilt the club wrote down how everything worked: rooms, food, funding, sponsors. His successor started with most answers in hand. Next time he'd start the doc on day one.

### The repeatable version

1. **Write the operations guide as you go.** Every problem you solve for the first time goes in one doc. At least:
   - Room reservation process
   - Food ordering and the funding request flow
   - Sponsor contact list, with each relationship's history, kept to the board
   - Where logins live: a password manager the board shares, never a doc
2. **Pick your successor by mid-year.** Your successor shadows you for at least one event, runs one alongside you, then runs one with you shadowing them.
3. **Recruit next year's board in the spring**, not after graduation.
4. **Ask every partner back within 30 days**, while the impression is warm. That habit is why the founding chapter had an Innovation Lab partner every term from Fall 2024 through Fall 2025.
5. **Tell the story.** Send winners and papers to your university's news office.

**In the dashboard:** add next year's board under **Your Chapter → Officers & Roles**: that's who can use the dashboard and who appears on your site. Events, the roster and the money history stay with the chapter, not with whoever set them up.
