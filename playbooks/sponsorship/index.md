# Sponsorship

**Every sponsor MAIC has landed started as a relationship, not a cold email — and the fastest way to make one is to put someone on stage.**

Sponsorship is the second thing a new chapter asks about and the thing there was no playbook for. This is that playbook: how MAIC's sponsors actually arrived, what to send, what to ask for, and how the money reaches your chapter without anyone fronting it on a personal card.

One structural thing to understand before any of it. Your chapter is a **sponsored project of the ALL Applied AI Network Foundation**, a 501(c)(3) (EIN 42-3604980). That means a company's gift is tax-deductible, the Foundation holds and accounts for the money, and your chapter directs how it's spent. You never take custody of the funds — which is exactly why the deduction works, and why a company's finance team can approve you without a fight.

---

## How we did it at MAIC

**Direct Supply, our primary sponsor, speaks and funds.** They send engineers to our speaker events regularly and they cover our food. The two are not separate relationships — the speaking is what keeps the funding warm, and the funding is what makes the speaking easy to ask for.

**Brady Corp came through a warm intro and a speaker slot.** An MSOE alum in Brady's engineering leadership reached out after hearing about Hacksgiving. They spoke at MAIC in mid-2024. The first real scoping meeting was July 2024, with their director of AI operations, their director of business operations, a product manager interested in AI, and two engineers — one also an MSOE alum. Ben opened by recapping what MAIC actually was, then offered three sponsorship pathways: speaker events, Innovation Labs, and Research Groups. Brady was interested in all three and landed on the Innovation Lab at $5K. They came back for Spring 2025.

**The hardest part was not the ask.** It was the NDA. Brady's CEO and president were nervous about competitors seeing student code, and we spent two weeks in legal back-and-forth in September that should have happened in week one. We've moved NDA discussion into the first meeting ever since.

**Not every sponsor is a company.** Our $6K Hacksgiving prize pool comes from Dr. Jeremy Kedziora's PieperPower Endowed Chair. Faculty with endowed funds, your dean's office, and your university's advancement office are all live options, and they move on a different calendar than corporate budgets.

## The repeatable version

### Where sponsors actually come from

In the order they've worked for us:

1. **Speakers you already hosted.** They've stood in your room and seen the students. Every sponsor conversation that started this way was warmer than any cold outreach. See the [Speaker Series playbook](https://all-ai-network.org/playbook.html?path=playbooks/speaker-series/index.md).
2. **Alumni at the company.** A warm intro from someone who went to your school is the single highest-conversion path we have. Your alumni office and LinkedIn will both find these.
3. **Faculty with endowed or discretionary funds.** Often faster than a corporate budget cycle and far less paperwork.
4. **The network.** Companies come to ALL Applied AI Network wanting to reach students rather than to any one club, so being a chapter puts you in conversations you didn't start. You can also pitch a prospect the whole network instead of one club at one school, which is a much lower bar for them to clear.

### The sequence

- **Ask for a speaker slot first, not money.** It is a smaller ask, it gets you in the room, and it gives them a reason to care before you name a number.
- **Thank them within 48 hours** of the event, with photos and the attendance number.
- **Follow up within 30 days**: "would your team want to do this again next year?" Brady came back because we asked while the impression was still warm.
- **Open the money conversation from what they saw**, not from a tier sheet. The tiers are how you close, not how you open.

### The first real meeting

- Get the NDA question on the table in **meeting one**. Not month two.
- Recap what your chapter actually is before you pitch anything — assume they know nothing.
- Offer **pathways, not a price**: a speaker slot, a sponsored project, a hackathon. Let them pick the shape, then talk money.
- Bring one concrete thing they'd be funding. "An Innovation Lab on a problem your team actually has" beats "general support."

### What to have ready before the meeting

- Your public funding page — tiers, what each one buys, and your sponsor wall.
- Proof you're a real charity: the Foundation's 501(c)(3) status and EIN. Their finance team will ask.
- One story about what students built. Not a mission statement.

### Setting it up in the dashboard

Configure your tiers and pitch under **Funding → Your page**, and record sponsors you already have so your wall reflects reality — a recorded sponsor occupies a real slot, which is what keeps your public availability honest. Money that arrives outside the platform (a cheque, a university transfer) still belongs in the ledger.

## What we'd do differently

We treated sponsorship as a fundraising activity for too long, when it is really a relationship activity that happens to produce money. The clubs that struggle are the ones that open with a tier sheet to a stranger; every sponsor we actually landed had already been in our room. We'd also have started the NDA conversation on day one — two weeks of September legal back-and-forth cost us real build time on the first Innovation Lab and it was entirely avoidable. And we under-asked at first: Brady was interested in all three pathways and we'd been prepared to pitch only one.
